# You Chose This Life Before You Were Born

## Intro

What if some part of your life was not random?

What if, before you were born, you already existed, and from that earlier state you took part in choosing the general kind of life you would live here?

According to *The Spirits' Book*, that is exactly what earthly life is.

Not a meaningless accident. Not a one-time test dropped on a brand new soul. But one chapter in a much longer journey.

In this view, the spirit exists before birth, survives death, and returns many times to bodily life. Before one of those returns, it reflects on what it still lacks, what faults it must repair, and what kind of trials might help it grow. Then it chooses, within the law of God, the general sort of life it needs.

That is a shocking idea. But if it were true, it would change how we understand suffering, injustice, freedom, responsibility, and even destiny itself.

## What Is Actually Chosen

The teaching does not say that a spirit chooses every tiny detail in advance.

It does not mean you selected every conversation, every illness, every accident, every betrayal, and every disappointment as if you were writing a script line by line.

The claim is more limited and more serious than that.

According to this doctrine, the spirit chooses the general kind of trial. It may choose poverty rather than wealth. Dependence rather than power. Obscurity rather than praise. A difficult family. A body with limitations. A life of service. A life of repair.

Then earthly events unfold within that framework through circumstances, the actions of other people, and your own continuing freedom.

This means your conditions may have meaning, but your moral choices are still truly yours.

That distinction matters.

Because if this teaching were taken to mean that every sin was prewritten, then responsibility would disappear. But *The Spirits' Book* does not teach that. It says the trial may be chosen, while the use you make of it remains a live moral question.

Your situation may be part of the lesson. Your response is where merit or failure enters.

## Why Would Any Spirit Choose Hardship

This is the hardest part to accept.

Why would anyone choose suffering?

The answer given is that the spirit sees more clearly before incarnation than it does while inside the body. On earth, we cling to comfort, reputation, appetite, success, and immediate relief. But in the freer state, the spirit sees farther.

It sees what still needs correction.

A spirit that once abused wealth may choose poverty, so it can learn humility, restraint, and trust.

A spirit that once ruled harshly may choose subjection, so it can feel from below what it once imposed from above.

A spirit that once caused physical suffering may choose a body that suffers, not because God delights in pain, but because lived experience can teach what abstract knowledge never could.

And not every difficult life is chosen as punishment.

Some are chosen for progress.

Some for repair.

Some for service.

The doctrine says a more advanced spirit may even accept a hard life as a mission, entering a painful environment in order to help others.

So in this view, hardship does not always mean guilt. Sometimes it means work. Sometimes correction. Sometimes courage. Sometimes love.

## Freedom, But Not Anarchy

Another important point is that this choice is not always equally free.

The more advanced the spirit, the more clearly it understands what kind of life it needs.

But a young, stubborn, or confused spirit may not choose wisely.

So the teaching says God may sometimes impose a life suited to correction when the spirit cannot yet judge well for itself.

That means there is freedom, but not chaos.

There is responsibility, but not abandonment.

There is divine law over the whole process.

This also means the doctrine is not a flattering fantasy in which every person imagines they heroically designed every pain in their biography.

No. Sometimes the spirit accepts. Sometimes it asks. Sometimes it resists. Sometimes it delays. Sometimes it must be directed.

The law remains merciful, but it is still a law.

## What This Changes

If you take this idea seriously, even as a possibility, several things shift at once.

First, suffering stops looking purely random.

It may still be painful. It may still be tragic. It may still involve wrongdoing by other people. But it is no longer meaningless by definition.

Second, earthly success becomes morally ambiguous.

Wealth may be a test, not a reward. Power may be a danger, not a sign of superiority. Hardship may be an opportunity, not proof of abandonment.

Third, the meaning of justice changes.

You no longer measure justice only by what happens in one short lifetime. You see each life as one scene in a much longer education.

But there is also a danger here.

This teaching should never be used to judge other people coldly.

It should not make us say, "That person must deserve this," or, "Their pain is just their karma."

The same body of teaching warns against that kind of certainty. The point is self-examination, humility, patience, and charity, not spiritual arrogance.

If someone suffers, your duty is still compassion.

If you suffer, the teaching invites you to ask not only, "Why is this happening to me?" but also, "What can this become in me?"

## Closing

So did you choose this life before you were born?

According to *The Spirits' Book*, you may have chosen at least its general kind, its major tests, and the sort of work your soul still needed.

That does not make life easy.

It does not erase grief.

It does not excuse evil.

But it does offer a radically different picture of existence.

You are not a being created yesterday and thrown into chaos.

You are an older spirit moving through a long moral education.

Your life may not be a prison built by fate.

It may be a field of growth entered, in some measure, with your own consent.

And if that is true, then the deepest question is not only what has happened to you.

It is what your soul came here to become.
